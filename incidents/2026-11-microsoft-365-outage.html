Was the Microsoft 365 outage a cyberattack?

Also reported as the Microsoft outage.

Is it a hack? The short answer

Debunked

All the public evidence says no. Microsoft says it was a technical failure, and the US government said on Nov 18 that the outage was not a cyberattack. Microsoft's final report, dated Nov 20, also says it was not a cyberattack.

Known so far

  • Most users could not reach their mailboxes, and parts of Teams, Copilot, SharePoint and OneDrive were also affected. evidence
  • Microsoft's preliminary report blames a latent defect from a July update, set off by extra traffic and reduced capacity. This is Microsoft's own finding. evidence

Not known yet

  • Why the Teams routing issue in Microsoft's US East region happened, and what the July update was meant to fix. The final report does not say.
  • Whether any independent body will publish its own finding on the cause. None has; the answer that it was not a cyberattack rests on Microsoft's report and the US government's statement.
  • Exactly how many users and organizations were affected.
  • When Microsoft's promised fixes, such as smaller fault zones and separate gateway pools for each workload, will be in place. Its final report dates several for early 2027.
  • Whether the phishing campaign stole any credentials. Microsoft says this has not been confirmed.

ConfirmedMicrosoft's final report: a July defect, set off by three conditions togetherMicrosoft's final report, dated Nov 20, blames a latent defect in a July update to its gateway servers, set off by capacity lost to planned maintenance, a Teams failover and peak North American traffic.

Why it supports The report's Executive Summary and Root Cause sections name the defect and the three conditions; GeekWire, KIRO 7, Neowin, The Guardian and GEroundtable report the same account.

The report says the defect caused lock contention inside an internal Windows process that used up the servers' CPU and memory. It hit only newer servers with more cores, and load testing on older hardware missed it. The defect does not apply to standard Windows deployments. Nearly 14% of gateway capacity was out of service for planned maintenance. At 1:07 AM UTC on Nov 12, a routing issue in the US East region moved Teams traffic worldwide onto the gateways Exchange Online uses. Compared with the preliminary report (c005), the final report names the maintenance as the reason for the missing capacity and adds peak traffic as a condition. This is Microsoft's own analysis. Microsoft's own copy needs a Microsoft 365 admin-center login (not checked: login required). GeekWire, an established outlet, reported on the final report on Nov 21 and gives the same account, including the July defect, the newer high-core servers, the 14% capacity lost to routine updates and the US East Teams routing issue. KIRO 7, a Seattle TV newsroom, also reported on the final report on Nov 21, quoting its line that Microsoft's testing "did not adequately represent the combination of hardware and traffic mix encountered during this incident". Neowin, an established tech news outlet, reported on the final report on Nov 22 with the same account: a July 2026 defect, reduced server capacity and extra Teams traffic set off a cascade that left more than 80% of the gateway servers unresponsive at one point. The Guardian, in a Nov 22 explainer on the recent run of outages, gives the same outline: an upgrade in July left in place a software bug that lay dormant for months until a string of coincidences triggered it and overwhelmed Microsoft's servers.
